# Mission and principles

Commonweal means shared wellbeing. We want everyone to be able to live comfortably without having to earn access to essentials through paid employment.

The intended living standard includes nutritious food, a suitable home, reliable energy, healthcare, connectivity, mobility and meaningful participation. Quality, reliability, choice, disability access, cultural needs and local circumstances belong in its definition. A survival threshold alone does not fulfil the mission.

This is a goal to research, design and work toward. Commonweal does not currently provide these services or promise that a particular policy or technology can fund them.

## Freedom and unconditional provision

People may choose paid work, enterprise, trade, care, learning, art or leisure. Business ownership and markets can coexist with unconditional essentials. The project should compare practical arrangements—including shared assets, services, income or dividends, and combinations—against the living standard, without making paid work a condition of belonging.

Free access still has production costs. Models must show who funds provision, who owns assets, how maintenance is sustained and how essential work remains staffed. Compensation, working conditions and physical capacity matter; unlimited automation or unpaid labour cannot be assumed.

## AI as a public-interest capability

AI is central to research, design, coding, simulation, optimisation and learning. Its capabilities should help people explore more possibilities and carry useful ideas into practice. The engineering task includes making that AI use sustainable and accessible.

AI outputs require scrutiny. A fluent explanation is not a measurement, a repeated simulation is not validation, and an optimised result is only as useful as its objectives, constraints and evidence. Record uncertainty and preserve contrary evidence.

## Environmental recovery

Assess the whole system: operational and lifecycle emissions, electricity at the time and place of use, hardware and infrastructure, materials, water, waste, land and ecosystem effects, including impacts outside New Zealand.

Develop clean supply, efficient use, repair, reuse and restoration alongside essential provision. Measure or clearly estimate AI's own footprint, identify unavailable provider data and account for rebound effects. Future savings must remain projections until observed. Financial gain must not hide environmental harm.

## Participation and authority

Communities and mana whenua shape objectives, governance, ownership and acceptable trade-offs. Māori data interests, authority and consent require engagement; publicly discoverable information is not automatically appropriate for unrestricted reuse.

Participation should work for renters, people without paid AI or reliable connectivity, and people with different access needs. Ordinary human interfaces and assisted participation are part of the design.

Publish who benefits, who pays, who may lose and what remains unknown. Do not collapse these differences into one opaque wellbeing score. Software supports decisions; affected people and legitimate decision-makers retain authority.

## Stewardship and sustainability

Keep core knowledge and access open. Recognise and fund the labour of research, review, maintenance and delivery through transparent arrangements. Disclose commissioning interests and conflicts; preserve results that disappoint a funder.

Begin in New Zealand, document what is local, and make useful methods adaptable elsewhere. Judge progress by reliable provision, environmental outcomes, inclusion and better practical decisions—not activity counts or model complexity.
